Railcar Storage: How Storage in Transit Works and What It Costs

Every rail shipper ends up with cars that have nowhere to go for a while. Where those cars sit decides what they cost. This guide covers what storage in transit is, how the railroads charge for cars held on their track, how private yard storage is billed, and what to ask a yard before you send it cars.

What storage in transit means

Storage in transit, usually shortened to SIT, is the practice of holding a railcar at a yard partway through its trip. The car leaves the origin plant loaded, stops at a storage yard, and waits there until the shipper orders it forward to a customer. The freight moves in two legs with a pause in the middle.

The pause exists because production and demand run on different schedules. A plant produces every day. Customers order in batches. The railcar absorbs the difference.

Storage also covers empty cars. Private cars sit between loads, between leases, during plant turnarounds, and through slow seasons. An empty car still occupies track, and whoever owns that track will charge for it.

The amount of equipment standing still at any one time can be large. In July 2020, rail analyst Jim Blaze wrote in FreightWaves that between 25% and 33% of the North American railcar fleet was in some kind of storage. That was a pandemic peak, but it shows how much of the fleet storage has to absorb when demand drops.

Three places a car can sit

Your own track

Track inside your plant costs nothing per day, and it is the first place cars go. It is also fixed. Once it is full, every additional car ends up in one of the other two places.

The railroad's track

A car held on the railroad's network is charged under the railroad's accessorial tariff. The rates are public. The table below shows what Union Pacific listed on its accessorial charges page on November 26, 2026.

ChargeTariff itemListed rate
Demurrage, railroad-owned carUP 6004, item 9605$240 per car per day in the serving area, $145 at private industry
Demurrage, loaded private carUP 6004, item 9605$145 per car per day in the serving area
Demurrage, loaded private car carrying hazardous material (non-TIH)UP 6004, item 9632$280 per car per day in the serving area
Private empty car storageUP 6004, item 9890$195 per net chargeable storage day

Union Pacific states on that page that its published tariff governs if the page and the tariff differ. Rates change, so check the current tariff before you rely on any of these figures.

The empty car charge works on debits and credits. According to Union Pacific's private empty car storage FAQ, one debit is assessed for every full or partial day a private empty car is in hold status on its network, and a credit is issued when a car is actually placed at the destination industry. The bill is total debits less total credits, multiplied by the storage fee.

The same FAQ states that debits are not assessed for private empty equipment on private track. That one sentence is the reason private storage yards exist.

Other Class I railroads publish their own versions of these charges. The rates and the rules differ by railroad, so read the tariff for the railroad that serves you.

A private storage yard

The third option is a yard that is not part of the railroad's own network and holds cars for a fee. Pricing is set by contract, not by tariff. Blaze notes that storage rates are usually contracted for a month or longer, and that car movements such as switching and pulling, along with maintenance services, are quoted separately.

A storage quote therefore has at least three parts: a daily rate per car, a switch charge each time a car moves in or out, and prices for any extra services such as cleaning, inspection or repair.

Running the comparison

The comparison is arithmetic once you have the numbers. Take ten empty private cars held on the railroad for 30 days at the $195 rate listed above. If every day is chargeable and no credits are earned, the bill is 10 cars x 30 days x $195, which is $58,500. That is an illustration, not a quote.

Now price the same ten cars at a private yard. Multiply the yard's daily rate by the same 300 car days, add the switch charges in and out, and add any extra freight needed to get the cars to the yard and back onto their route.

The comparison in one line

Railroad daily charge x chargeable days, against yard daily rate x days, plus switch charges, plus added freight. Storage at a private yard makes sense when the second total is lower.

Two things move the answer. The first is length of stay. Switch charges are paid once per move, so they weigh heavily on a short stay and very little on a long one. The second is location. A yard far off the car's route adds freight cost and transit time in both directions.

Loaded hazardous materials cars are a separate question

Federal rules limit where a loaded hazardous materials car can wait. Under 49 CFR 174.14, a carrier must forward each shipment of hazardous materials within 48 hours of accepting it at origin or receiving it at any yard, transfer station or interchange point. Saturdays, Sundays and holidays are excluded from the count. The same section says a tank car loaded with flammable gas, poisonous gas or flammable liquid may not be received and held at any point in a way that defeats that requirement.

What matters next is how the storage track is classified. Under 49 CFR 171.1, a rail car stays in transportation until it is delivered to a private track or siding, and the Hazardous Materials Regulations do not apply to storage of a rail car on private track after delivery. A car that holds hazardous material and is stored on track that does not meet the definition of private track in 49 CFR 171.8 is treated as storage incidental to movement. That car remains under the regulations even if it has reached the destination shown on the shipping document.

Before you send loaded hazardous materials cars to any yard, ask how its storage track is classified and which obligations follow from that. This section is a summary of the rules, not legal advice. Confirm the details with your own compliance team.

What to ask a storage yard

  • Which railroads serve the yard? Ask how cars are interchanged and whether your cars can reach the yard without an extra carrier in the route.
  • How fast can a car be released? Ask how often the yard switches and how much notice an outbound order needs.
  • What is in the rate? Get the daily rate, the switch charges, the minimum term and any minimum car count in writing.
  • How is the storage track classified for hazardous materials? The answer decides which rules apply to your loaded cars.
  • How are cars inspected and secured? Blaze recommends that hatches, protective housing covers and bottom outlet valves be inspected daily to weekly, and that yards limit the risk of tampering.
  • Can repairs happen while the cars sit? Blaze points out that repairs are cheaper while cars are gathered in one place. Cutting a car out for repair just before it moves can add one or two switching charges, which he put at $400 or more each time in 2020.
  • What reporting will you get? You should receive an inventory by car number with the dates each car arrived and left.

The short version

A car that cannot go to your own track will be charged for wherever it sits. Railroad charges are published. Union Pacific's listed rates in November 2026 were $145 to $280 per car per day for the demurrage items above and $195 per day for private empty car storage.

A private yard is priced by contract. Get the daily rate and the switch charges in writing, then run the comparison with your real car count and your real number of days. For loaded hazardous materials cars, settle the track classification question first.
