Railcar Switching: Why Cars Miss a Switch and How to Prevent It

The last move of a rail shipment is made by a local crew that serves each customer on set days. When that move is missed, the car waits for the next service day. This guide covers what a switch is, how a federal rule defined a missed one, why switches are missed, what a miss costs, and what to do before each service day.

What a switch is

A switch is the local move that places a railcar at a facility or takes it away. Railroads call a placement a spot and a pick-up a pull. Those are the terms the Surface Transportation Board used when it wrote a service standard for local switching in 2024, in 49 CFR 1145.2.

Cars do not go from the long-haul train straight to your track. Union Pacific, for example, holds each customer's cars in what it calls a Serving Area. Its Chargeable Events FAQ defines the Serving Area as the location where cars are held before placement at the customer's facility. A local crew then moves cars between the Serving Area and the customer.

The same FAQ describes two kinds of customer. A Spot-on-Arrival customer's cars may be placed without placement instructions. An Order-In customer's cars are not placed until the customer has given placement instructions.

Local service runs on deadlines. The 2024 federal rule defined the cut-off time as the deadline for requesting service during a service window, set by the railroad's own protocol (49 CFR 1145.1). Under the rule, a window counted as planned only if the customer had requested service by the cut-off time.

How a federal rule defined a missed switch

The Surface Transportation Board's 2024 rule on reciprocal switching included a performance standard called industry spot and pull. It measured a railroad's success in performing spots and pulls at a shipper's or receiver's facility during the planned service window. A railroad met the standard with a success rate of 85% or more over 12 consecutive weeks. The standard did not apply to unit trains or intermodal traffic.

The rule also set out who was responsible for a miss.

  • If the railroad cancelled a service window, other than at the customer's request, the window counted as a failure.
  • Failing to spot cars that the customer had ordered in by the cut-off time counted as a failure.
  • A spot-on-arrival car counted as missed only if it had reached the local yard and was ready for local service before the cut-off time.
  • When the customer caused the railroad to miss a planned window, the window was not counted as a miss.

The rule is no longer in effect. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit vacated it in a decision issued on July 8, 2025, saying the Board had exceeded its authority, as FreightWaves reported the next day. We describe the standard here because its definitions show how a regulator divided responsibility for a missed switch between the railroad and the customer.

Why a switch is missed on the customer's side

Several causes are within the customer's control. Union Pacific's published rules show each one.

  • The switch was not requested ahead of time. In a 2022 customer notice, Union Pacific asked customers to submit switch requests through its Shipment Management tool, because a request made in advance appears on the crew's daily work schedule.
  • The cars were not ordered in. For an Order-In customer, loaded cars that arrive in the Serving Area go into constructive placement status. The customer is required to order them in for placement.
  • There was no room on the track. Union Pacific counts Available Capacity as the customer's track capacity minus the cars on that track that have not been released. If no Available Capacity exists, the cars remain in the Serving Area.
  • Finished cars were not released. Releasing an empty car creates Available Capacity. Under the FAQ, unloading and releasing the cars on your track triggers the placement of the loaded cars held in the Serving Area, up to your Available Capacity.
  • The facility was not ready for the crew. Union Pacific lists a charge called Not Prepared for Service for manifest traffic. A crew cannot work a track behind a locked gate, a blocked track, or a car that is still connected to unloading equipment.

When the railroad misses

Not every miss is the customer's. Under Union Pacific's system, a customer that is not on a team track receives Capacity Credits if it has Available Capacity and cars in the Serving Area and does not receive a switch, or receives a switch for fewer cars than its Available Capacity. Two Capacity Credits are issued per car in the Serving Area, up to the maximum Available Capacity.

Order-In customers who ask for a specific car are also covered. If the railroad delivers a different car, or places no car, two Placement Credits are issued per rail car.

What a missed switch costs

The first cost is time on the clock. For customers that are not on a team track, Union Pacific issues one debit per day for each chargeable car. The count starts at 12:00 midnight after the car arrives in the Serving Area or is actually placed at the facility, whichever is earlier. A car that stays in the Serving Area because there is no room for it is subject to that daily debit while it waits. Our guide to demurrage covers how the railcar clock works.

The second cost is the published charges. Union Pacific's accessorial charges page listed these amounts on November 26, 2026.

The third cost is at the plant. Production waits for product, and the trucks or crews booked for the unloading have nothing to work. If cars regularly arrive faster than you can take them, our guide to railcar storage compares the places a car can wait and what each one costs.

Where a yard with its own switching fits

A railroad's local crew serves many customers on a fixed schedule. A private railyard that does its own switching can move a car between its storage track and its transfer track without waiting for the railroad's next service day. The railroad delivers and collects cars at the yard. The moves inside the yard are the yard's own.

Before each service day

  • Know your service days and cut-off time. Ask your railroad's local service contact, and post both where the shipping office can see them.
  • Release finished cars before the cut-off. A car that has not been released still counts against your capacity.
  • Order in the next cars. If you are an Order-In customer, order up to the room you will have after the pull.
  • Request any extra switch in advance. The crew can only plan for work it can see on its schedule.
  • Make the track ready. Disconnect the cars, clear the track and open the gate before the crew arrives.
  • Check the record afterward. Compare what was spotted and pulled with what you requested. If the railroad missed a switch while you had room, check that the credits were issued.

The short version

A switch is a spot or a pull made by a local crew on a set day. The customer-side causes of a miss are a late request, cars that were not ordered in, no room on the track because finished cars were not released, and a facility that is not ready. Each one leaves the car waiting for the next service day while the daily debit continues.
