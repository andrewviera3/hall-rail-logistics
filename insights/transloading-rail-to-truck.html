Transloading: How Freight Moves Between Railcar and Truck

Most plants that buy or sell bulk product are not on a rail line. Transloading is how they use rail anyway. This guide covers how a transload works, how many trucks a railcar takes, what equipment each kind of product needs, the federal rules for hazardous materials transfers, and what to ask a terminal before you route cars to it.

What transloading is

Transloading is the transfer of product between a railcar and a truck at a terminal that has both track and road access. Rail carries the long haul. The truck covers the first or last miles to a plant that has no track of its own.

Union Pacific describes the arrangement in its rail shipping FAQ. If the origin is not rail served, trucks deliver the product to a transload facility, where it is transferred to a train. If the destination has no track either, the product is transferred back to trucks for final delivery.

Transloading is different from intermodal. In intermodal service the container moves from train to truck and the product stays inside it. In a transload the product itself is moved from one vehicle to the other.

One railcar means several trucks

A railcar holds much more than a truck. According to Union Pacific, one rail car can hold three to four truckloads of freight. The same page explains why. Most rail cars can have a gross weight of up to 286,000 pounds, and the federal gross vehicle weight limit for a truck on the Interstate system is 80,000 pounds, a limit also listed by the Federal Highway Administration.

That ratio drives the planning. Unloading one railcar takes three to four truck trips, so an order of ten railcars takes 30 to 40 truck trips. The trucks, the drivers and the delivery appointments have to be arranged before the cars arrive.

When they are not, the cars wait, and waiting cars cost money. Our guides to demurrage and railcar storage cover what that waiting costs and where it is cheapest.

How a transload works

The details change with the product, but a typical rail to truck transfer follows the same steps.

  1. The car is placed. The railroad or the terminal's own switch crew spots the railcar on the transfer track.
  2. The car is checked. The crew confirms the car number against the paperwork and inspects the seals, valves and fittings.
  3. The car is secured and connected. Brakes are set, the track is protected, and the transfer equipment is connected.
  4. The product is transferred. The quantity is measured by meter or by weighing the truck.
  5. The truck leaves. The truck is sealed and released with its paperwork. The railcar is either worked again until it is empty or released back to the railroad.

Equipment depends on the product

A terminal built for one kind of freight cannot always handle another. Union Pacific's guide to loading and unloading rail cars describes the usual method for each car type.

RailcarTypical freightHow it is unloaded
Covered hopperDry bulk such as plastic pellets, grain and cementBy gravity through gates at the bottom of the car. Food-grade hoppers can also be unloaded by pneumatic transfer.
BoxcarPackaged and palletized goodsMost often by forklift through the door in the middle of the car.
FlatcarMachinery, lumber and other large piecesMost commonly by crane or forklift.
Coil carSteel coilsTypically by overhead crane or forklift.
Tank carLiquids and gasesThrough the bottom outlet valve or the top fittings, with hoses connecting the car to the truck.

Ask a terminal which of these it is set up for before you assume it can take your product.

Hazardous materials: the transfer is regulated

Federal hazardous materials rules treat transloading as a defined activity. 49 CFR 171.8 defines it as the transfer of a hazardous material from one bulk packaging to another, or between bulk and non-bulk packaging, for the purpose of continuing the movement in commerce.

49 CFR 174.67 sets the rules for tank car transloading. Among them:

  • Unloading must be performed by hazmat employees who have been properly instructed in unloading hazardous materials.
  • The handbrake must be applied and at least one wheel blocked so the car cannot move.
  • Access to the track must be secured against other rail equipment, for example with locked switches or derails.
  • Caution signs bearing the word STOP in white letters on a blue background must be displayed on the track or on the cars.
  • The facility operator must keep written safety procedures where the employees doing the transfer can reach them immediately.
  • For the whole period of unloading, and while unloading equipment is attached, the car must be attended by a designated hazmat employee with an unobstructed view, or monitored by a signaling system that a designated employee observes.

A terminal that handles hazardous materials should be able to show you each of these in practice. This section is a summary of the rules, not legal advice. Confirm the details with your own compliance team.

What to ask a transload terminal

  • Which railroads serve the terminal? Ask how often cars are switched in and out.
  • What products is it set up for? Match the equipment to your freight using the table above.
  • How is cross contamination prevented? Ask whether hoses and transfer equipment are dedicated to one product or cleaned between products.
  • How is quantity measured? Ask whether the terminal uses a meter or a truck scale, and what record you receive for each load.
  • How many cars and trucks can it work in a day? The answer tells you whether the terminal can keep up with your volume.
  • Where do cars wait? Ask whether there is storage track on site for cars that are not yet unloaded.
  • What are the truck loading hours? Your carriers need to know when they can arrive.
  • What reporting will you get? You should receive inventory by car number and a record of every truck loaded.

The short version

Transloading lets a plant with no rail spur buy or sell by rail. Plan the trucks first, because each railcar takes three to four of them. Match the terminal's equipment to your product. For hazardous materials, ask to see the written procedures and how the transfer is attended.
